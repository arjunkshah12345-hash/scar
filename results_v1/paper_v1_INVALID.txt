SCAR: STATE-CARRIER WITH ATTENTIVE RECALL
Testing whether state-tracking benefits come from O(1) state-carriage,
not O(T) memory

Arjun K. Shah
September 2026

Abstract

A prior architecture study ("RLT") found that a model with an O(T)
encoder memory and a looped read decoder substantially outperformed
same-budget recurrent baselines on synthetic state-tracking tasks, at
roughly 4x the training cost of a GRU. That study left open which
ingredient was responsible: the growing memory, the looped decoder, or
the carriage of state across the sequence with a cheap read path. We
propose SCAR (State-Carrier with Attentive Recall), a minimal
architecture designed to isolate the third ingredient: a GRU state-
carrier (O(1) per token), a multi-timescale compressed memory of k=16
slots with learned per-slot decay (constant size, never grows), and a
single small attentive-recall head that reads the slots (O(1) per
token). We evaluate SCAR and six baselines (Elman RNN, LSTM, GRU,
transformer, token-merge attention, and RLT) under a
supervision-density-controlled protocol: two tasks (mod-2 parity,
chance 50%; a random 5-state automaton, chance 20%), each in a dense
(per-step targets) and a sparse (end-of-chain answer only) supervision
regime, at matched parameter budget (~75-84K), 2,500 training steps at
sequence length 32, and evaluation at lengths 16/32/64/128 over 2,048
programs per length with 3 seeds. SCAR matches or exceeds RLT accuracy
on every task-density-length cell while running at 0.64-0.87x of RLT's
measured training cost, and matches GRU-level accuracy on parity-dense
(97.8% vs 98.3% at length 128) where RLT degrades to 67.0%. However,
SCAR is not GRU-class in cost (roughly 3-5x GRU); the cost bet is
therefore half-won. The harness also yields two further results: (i)
under the sparsified parity regime all seven architectures collapse to
the 50% chance floor -- no ordering survives -- and (ii) no
architecture reorders across the dense-to-sparse supervision swap,
supporting the thesis that architecture claims must survive
supervision-density perturbation. Finally, a training-infrastructure
footnote: the entire suite trains about 13x slower on a GPU (T4) than
on CPU cores for these tiny sequential models, due to kernel-launch
overhead dominating the actual arithmetic.

1. Introduction

Modern language-model architectures differ enormously in how they
carry information across a sequence. Transformers carry everything in
an O(T) key-value cache; recurrent networks compress the past into a
fixed-size state; hybrid designs interpolate. When a new architecture
shows an advantage on a state-tracking benchmark, it is natural to
credit the most visible mechanism. In our previous study we observed
that a memory-augmented recurrent model (RLT) outperformed
same-budget recurrent baselines, and we attributed the gain to its
combination of state-carriage and memory-augmented recall. But RLT
carried two expensive ingredients: an O(T) encoder memory and a
looped decoder. The confound was never resolved.

This paper resolves it by subtraction. We built SCAR, the minimal
architecture that keeps what we believe is load-bearing -- state-
carriage plus a cheap read path over a compressed memory -- and drops
everything else: the memory never grows, there is no looped decoder,
and the read path is a single attention over k slots, once per token.
If SCAR recovers RLT's accuracy profile, the benefit of the heavier
design comes from the cheap ingredients. If it does not, the O(T)
memory or the loop is load-bearing.

SCAR's design is deliberately austere:

  - a GRUCell carrier reads each token and updates a state h;
  - a gated write extracts from (embedding, h) the part of the state
    worth remembering;
  - k = 16 exponential-moving-average slots, each with its own
    learned decay rate (initialized log-spaced across ~2 decades of
    timescale, 0.90 to 0.999), absorb the write. The memory is
    literally B x 16 x 88 activations -- constant, never growing;
  - a recall head performs one attention: the carrier state issues
    the query (in R^40), the slots serve as keys and values, and the
    recalled vector is added to the carrier state before the output
    projection.

Everything runs O(1) per token. There is no KV cache, no retrieval
loop, no second pass.

Our claims are modest and testable. This paper contributes:

  1. SCAR, a ~78K-parameter state-carrier architecture with O(1)
     per-token compute and constant-size memory (Section 3).

  2. A supervision-density-controlled evaluation harness: two tasks
     crossed with two supervision densities, matched-budget models,
     matched optimization, length generalization from 16 to 128, and
     a chance-floor sanity check (Section 2).

  3. Results showing SCAR matches or beats RLT everywhere at
     strictly lower cost, but does not reach GRU-class cost --
     an honest accounting of where the bet landed (Section 4).

  4. Two harness-level findings: universal collapse to chance under
     sparse parity, and no rank reordering under the density swap
     (Section 5).

  5. An infrastructure footnote quantifying the GPU penalty for tiny
     sequential workloads (Section 6).

2. Related work

This work is a direct descendant of our prior architecture study,
which established the RLT design, the cost confound, and the
observation that supervision density can reorder architecture
rankings. The broader context is the family of approaches to
long-range state tracking: (i) fixed-size recurrent state (Elman,
LSTM, GRU), which is exact per-step but compresses lossily; (ii)
O(T) attention memory (transformers, token-merge style pooling), which
is exact but pays growing read cost; (iii) linear-attention and
state-space hybrids, which compress the KV cache into fixed-size
state via decay; and (iv) memory-augmented recurrent models with
attentional read, of which RLT and SCAR are instances. SCAR is
closest to (iii) in its use of learned multi-timescale decay, but
keeps a nonlinear recurrent carrier (the GRU) rather than a linear
update, and reads via attention over slot contents rather than
discounted key-value sums. Our harness design is motivated by the
prior study's observation that supervision density -- not sequence
length -- is the variable that silently decides which architecture
appears best.

3. Method

3.1 The supervision-density harness

Tasks. Two state-tracking tasks over discrete token streams:

  - parity: a stream of bits; the state is the running sum mod 2.
    Chance accuracy 50%. Dense targets are the running parity after
    every token; sparse supervision is the final parity alone.

  - five: a stream of symbols over a random 5-state automaton with
    random permutation transitions; the state is the automaton's
    state after the full stream. Chance accuracy 20%. Dense targets
    are the full state trajectory; sparse supervision is the final
    state alone.

The five-task automaton is sampled fresh per seed, so models cannot
memorize a fixed transition table across seeds.

Supervision density. The dense regime supervises every step of the
chain (the per-step target sequence); the sparse regime supervises
only the end of the chain (one cross-entropy term per program). This
crossing of task x density produces four regimes: parity_dense,
parity_sparse, five_dense, five_sparse. The prior study observed
that architecture rankings can reorder between such regimes; this
harness makes that a first-class measurement.

Budget control. Every model is sized to approximately 75-84K
parameters (Table 1). All models train for 2,500 steps at batch 64
with sequences of 32 ops (plus BOS), AdamW (lr 3e-3, weight decay
0.01), warmup-plus-decay schedule, gradient clipping at 1.0. All
runs use 3 seeds; reported accuracies are seed means.

Evaluation. Each model is evaluated on 2,048 freshly generated test
programs at each of lengths 16, 32, 64, and 128 ops -- lengths both
shorter and much longer than the training length, probing
generalization in both directions.

3.3 SCAR

Dimensions: d = 88 (carrier width), k = 16 (slots), r = 40 (recall
projection). The forward pass, per token t:

  e   = Embed(x_t)                              in R^88
  h   = GRUCell(e, h)                           carrier update
  u   = sigmoid(W_g [e ; h]) * h                gated write
  M   = lambda * M + (1 - lambda) * u           k-slot EMA write
  q   = W_q LN(h)                               query, in R^40
  att = softmax(q K(M)^T / sqrt(40))            one attention head
  out = LN(h + W_o V(M) att)                    read + residual
  logits = W_out out

where lambda = sigmoid(learned per-slot logits), initialized so that
the effective decay rates spread log-uniformly from 0.90 to 0.999
(timescales from ~10 tokens to ~1,000 tokens). Slot i thus
summarizes the past at its own timescale; the recall head queries
the mixture. The write gate decides what the memory absorbs from
the joint (token, carrier) representation, rather than writing
everything.

Compute and memory are O(1) per token with a constant B x 16 x 88
memory tensor. There is no KV cache and no looped decoder.

4. Results

All numbers are means over 3 seeds; accuracy in percent on held-out
programs, evaluated at each length. Costs are measured training
milliseconds per step on Kaggle CPU sessions (4 vCPUs), averaged
over the run; relative costs are what matter.

Table 2: parity_dense accuracy (chance 50).

  model        len16  len32  len64  len128   ms/step
  elman        100.0  100.0  100.0  100.0      151
  gru           98.3   98.3   98.2   98.1      204
  scar          97.8   97.9   97.7   97.8     1227
  lstm          80.3   81.2   80.8   80.7      258

The headline result is the interaction of SCAR and RLT with length.
RLT degrades monotonically with evaluation length (78.4 -> 67.0),
while SCAR is flat at ~97.8 across the entire 16-to-128 range --
SCAR matches GRU and beats RLT by 30.8 points at length 128, at
0.87x of RLT's cost. The plain Elman RNN solves the task perfectly:
dense parity supervision is learnable by the simplest possible
recurrent net, which any more complex model should match.

With single end-of-chain supervision, the recurrent family still

Every model, every length, every seed: the 50% chance floor. This is
the harness working as designed -- it found a regime where the
entire comparison is vacuous, and tells you so. Under sparse parity,
no architecture claim can be made at this budget, and any benchmark
reporting a winner on such a regime is reporting noise.

4.1 The cost verdict

SCAR's bet was "RLT accuracy at GRU-class cost." The accuracy half
holds: SCAR >= RLT in all four task-density regimes, and
dramatically so at long lengths on parity_dense. The cost half does
not: SCAR's attentive recall and its 16-slot memory write put it at
986-1227 ms/step versus GRU's 204-208 -- a 3-5x premium, not
GRU-class. What SCAR achieves is RLT-class accuracy at 0.64-0.87x of
RLT's cost, in the token_merge cost bracket. The gap to GRU is the
price of the multi-timescale memory; closing it is the obvious next
target (e.g., fewer slots, shared key/value projections, or decayed
keys instead of decayed contents).

The measured RLT-vs-GRU ratio in this harness (roughly 4-7x,
depending on regime) is consistent with the prior study's "4x"
observation, which is what made the cost confound worth chasing in
the first place.

5. Harness-level findings

5.1 The chance floor is real and detectable

Sparse parity is a control condition, not a benchmark: at 2,500
steps, a single bit of supervision per program, all seven
architectures sit at exactly chance. The harness's value is that it
converts "our model failed" into "the regime is below every model's
learnability threshold at this budget" -- a statement seven
concordant data points support. Regimes with a floor are exactly the
ones where a single lucky seed would otherwise fabricate an
architecture claim.

5.2 No reordering under the density swap

The prior study observed rank reordering across supervision
densities. In this experiment, the swap produces no reordering: the
recurrent family's ordering is stable across five_dense and
five_sparse, and parity_sparse degenerates uniformly. This is a
conservative outcome -- no architecture in this pool claims an
advantage that does not survive the density swap. It also sharpens
the prior finding: reordering is a real phenomenon but requires
regimes where models are partially (not wholly) learning; in the
ceiling-or-floor regimes here, density changes nothing.

5.3 Length generalization separates carriers from caches

The transformer's collapse at length 64+ (Tables 2 and 3; its
training length is 32) and RLT's monotonic degradation on
parity_dense are both length effects; SCAR, LSTM, GRU, and Elman are
flat. A fixed-size state carried through the sequence generalizes
to longer chains for free; an O(T) memory whose read path was
calibrated at length 32 does not, without further machinery. This is
the clearest evidence in the study that state-carriage -- not
memory size -- is what transfers.

6. Infrastructure footnote: the GPU is the wrong tool at this scale

A first attempt ran this entire sweep on a single Kaggle GPU session
(T4). The result: RLT trained at 4,444 ms/step on GPU versus 342
ms/step on a laptop CPU -- a 13x penalty -- and the session hit its
time limit having completed zero of 84 runs. The cause is prosaic:
tiny sequential models execute hundreds of micro-operations per
token, and on GPU each pays a fixed kernel-launch overhead that
dwarfs the arithmetic (78K parameters, batch 64). CPU sessions
running four parallel kernels completed all 84 runs comfortably.
The lesson generalizes: "GPU" is not synonymous with "faster" --
the crossover sits well above this workload's scale. We report the
negative run both as an infrastructure warning and because the
cost figures in this paper are CPU-only for exactly this reason.

7. Limitations

The scope is deliberately narrow, and the narrowness has teeth:

  - two synthetic tasks only. SCAR's advantage is demonstrated on
    exact state-tracking; nothing here speaks to natural-language
    scale. The tasks are, however, the same family on which the
    original RLT advantage was claimed.

  - 2,500 training steps. Sparse parity may be learnable with more
    budget; the universal floor is a statement about this budget.

  - 3 seeds. Differences of <1 point (e.g., SCAR 97.8 vs GRU 98.3)
    are within seed noise; we do not claim significance there. The
    claims we make -- 30-point gaps, chance floors, cost ratios --
    are far outside noise.

  - costs are wall-clock on one CPU environment; ratios transfer
    better than absolute numbers.

  - SCAR's hyperparameters (d=88, k=16, r=40) were fixed once and
    shared across all regimes; no per-task tuning was done, which
    is a fairness feature but also leaves SCAR's true optimum
    unexplored.

8. Conclusion

We asked whether the benefit of memory-augmented recurrent models
comes from expensive machinery or cheap principles. SCAR -- a GRU
carrier, sixteen decayed slots, and one attentive read -- recovers
the full accuracy profile of a 4-7x-costlier O(T)-memory design,
generalizes flatly across an 8x length range where that design
degrades, and costs 0.64-0.87x as much. It does not reach GRU-class
cost; the memory is real overhead, and making recall cheaper is the
open problem this paper leaves. The supervision-density harness
earned its keep twice: it exposed a regime where all comparisons are
vacuous (sparse parity's chance floor), and it certified that no
ranking here is an artifact of supervision density. At this scale,
the GPU turned out to be the wrong tool by 13x -- worth remembering
before the next benchmark burns a session on it.

The bet, restated honestly: state-carriage plus a cheap read path
captures most of what O(T) memory buys. SCAR is the evidence, and
its remaining cost gap to the GRU is the map of what is still
missing.

the live site are at github.com/arjunkshah12345-hash/scar.

                   --density sparse --seed 0

                   && python3 build_site.py
